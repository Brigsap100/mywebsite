Roof Systems

Built-Up Roofing (BUR) Systems

The original commercial roof: multiple plies of asphalt and reinforcement built up into one thick, redundant waterproofing system — protection measured in layers, not one membrane.

The basics

What is built-up roofing?

Built-up roofing is exactly what the name says: a roof built up on site from alternating layers of hot asphalt (or cold-applied adhesive) and reinforcing felts or fabrics. Three, four, or more plies are mopped or applied one over another, then finished with a protective surfacing — traditionally embedded gravel, or a mineral cap sheet or reflective coating.

Where a single-ply roof is one membrane doing all the work, a BUR is a redundant assembly. A puncture or defect in one ply still has multiple waterproof layers beneath it. This is the oldest continuously used commercial roof system in America, and the reason is simple: layered redundancy works.

Its close cousin, modified bitumen, applies the same asphalt-based logic in factory-made rolls — we install both, and often recommend hybrid assemblies that combine BUR plies with a modified cap sheet.

Best-use cases

Where BUR excels

BUR isn't the newest system on the market — it's the one that's outlasted every trend. On the right building, nothing else offers the same margin for error.

  • Redundancy — multiple waterproof plies mean a single defect rarely becomes a leak
  • High rooftop traffic — mechanical yards, frequently serviced equipment, and maintenance-heavy roofs benefit from the thick, tough, gravel-surfaced assembly
  • Puncture and impact resistance well beyond a single membrane layer
  • Buildings where owners prioritize long-term durability over lowest first cost
  • Reflective cap sheets and coatings let modern BUR meet cool-roof requirements while keeping the multi-ply core
The honest part

Where BUR is not the right choice

BUR's strengths come with real trade-offs. Here's what we tell owners before they sign anything.

Weight-limited structures

A multi-ply assembly with gravel surfacing is heavy. Some buildings — especially lightweight-deck structures or those already carrying old roof layers — can't take the load without structural review. A structural check comes before a BUR proposal, not after.

Odor- and disruption-sensitive occupancies

Hot-asphalt installation produces fumes and odor. Around hospitals, schools, food production, and occupied offices, that matters. Cold-applied BUR reduces the issue, but sometimes a heat-welded single-ply like TPO is simply the kinder choice for building occupants.

Over wet insulation — never

The same rule as every system: no new roof, BUR included, goes over saturated insulation. Redundant plies over a wet substrate just hide the problem longer. Moisture surveys first, always.

One more consideration: because BUR is built on site ply by ply, it is more installation-dependent than factory-made membranes. A great BUR crew produces a superb roof; a careless one builds the defects in permanently. Ask any bidder who will actually be on your roof.

Common questions

Built-up roofing FAQ

Is BUR outdated compared to single-ply?

No — it's a different tool. Single-ply won market share on speed and cost, but BUR's multi-ply redundancy and traffic tolerance remain unmatched on the right building. Plenty of owners with maintenance-heavy roofs still specify BUR deliberately, and for good reason.

How long does a BUR roof last?

With sound installation and regular maintenance, built-up roofs deliver decades of service — many of the oldest roofs still performing in the West are BUR. Lifespan depends on ply count, surfacing, drainage, and upkeep.

Will the installation smell? Can my building stay occupied?

Hot-asphalt work produces noticeable odor during installation. We manage it with staging, scheduling, and air-intake coordination, and cold-applied options reduce it further. Most buildings remain occupied during the work — we plan that with you up front.

Can BUR meet California cool-roof (Title 24) requirements?

Yes. Modern BUR systems are finished with reflective cap sheets or coatings that meet cool-roof requirements while keeping the multi-ply protection underneath. Gravel-surfaced assemblies have their own compliance paths — we'll walk you through what applies to your building.
