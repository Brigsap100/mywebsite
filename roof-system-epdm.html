EPDM Roofing Systems

A synthetic rubber membrane with one of the longest real-world track records of any low-slope roof system — simple, flexible, and proven across decades of weather.

The basics

What is EPDM?

EPDM (ethylene propylene diene monomer) is a synthetic rubber membrane — usually black, supplied in very large sheets — that has been protecting low-slope commercial roofs for generations. Unlike thermoplastics such as TPO, EPDM seams aren't heat-welded; sheets are joined with seam tapes and adhesives engineered for the material.

EPDM can be installed three ways: fully adhered (glued to the substrate for a clean, wind-resistant finish), mechanically fastened (screwed through to the deck), or ballasted (loose-laid and held down by rounded stone or pavers). The right method depends on your deck, wind exposure, and budget.

The membrane's defining trait is elasticity — it stretches and returns as the building moves and temperatures swing, which is a big part of why so many EPDM roofs from decades ago are still in service.

Best-use cases

Where EPDM excels

EPDM is the system many roofers point to when asked what has simply lasted. Its strengths are durability, flexibility, and a repair path that stays simple for the life of the roof.

  • Proven longevity — decades of documented field performance, one of the longest track records in low-slope roofing
  • Excellent weather resistance — handles UV, ozone, hail, and extreme temperature swings without becoming brittle
  • High elasticity — accommodates building movement and thermal cycling that would stress stiffer membranes
  • Very large sheet sizes — fewer seams on big, open roofs means fewer potential leak points
  • Straightforward, well-understood repairs — a mature system that any qualified crew can maintain for its whole life
The honest part

Where EPDM is not the right choice

EPDM's track record is real — but so are its trade-offs. Here's where we'd steer you elsewhere.

Hot, cooling-dominated climates

Standard EPDM is black, and a black roof absorbs heat. In much of California and Nevada that works against your cooling bill and against Title 24 cool-roof requirements. White EPDM exists, but a reflective thermoplastic like TPO is often the more natural fit for these climates.

Over wet insulation

As with any membrane, installing EPDM over saturated insulation is never right. Moisture surveys come first; wet material comes out. No system performs on a compromised substrate.

Grease and petroleum exposure

Oils, solvents, and restaurant grease degrade EPDM rubber. Roofs below kitchen exhaust or with petroleum-product exposure need a different membrane or dedicated protection at exposure points.

One more honest note: EPDM's seams rely on adhesives and tapes rather than welds. Modern seam tape is excellent, but seam quality still comes down to surface prep and workmanship — another reason installer quality matters more than brand.

Common questions

EPDM roofing FAQ

How long does an EPDM roof last?

EPDM has one of the longest documented service records in commercial roofing — decades of proven performance when installed correctly and maintained. Exact lifespan depends on membrane thickness, attachment method, exposure, and upkeep.

Is a black roof a problem in California or Nevada?

It can be. Black EPDM absorbs solar heat, which raises cooling loads and can conflict with Title 24 cool-roof requirements on low-slope commercial buildings. We'll tell you honestly whether EPDM, white EPDM, or a reflective system like TPO fits your building and code situation.

Can an aging EPDM roof be repaired instead of replaced?

Often, yes — that's one of EPDM's genuine strengths. Seams can be re-taped and details refreshed if the field membrane is sound. We assess the whole assembly, including the insulation below, before recommending life extension versus replacement.

What's the difference between ballasted and adhered EPDM?

Ballasted systems are loose-laid and weighted with stone — fast and economical, but heavy and harder to inspect for leaks. Fully adhered systems are glued down for a cleaner, lighter, more wind-resistant roof. Your deck's structural capacity and wind exposure usually decide it.
