Article 01 — Membrane selection

TPO vs. EPDM: choosing a single-ply membrane for California and Nevada climates

Walk any commercial district in California or Nevada and most of the low-slope roofs you can't see are covered in one of two single-ply membranes: TPO (thermoplastic polyolefin) or EPDM (ethylene propylene diene monomer, the familiar black rubber roof). Both are proven systems. The right choice depends far less on which material is "better" and far more on your building, your climate exposure, and how the roof will be used and maintained.

Start with the sun. TPO is typically white or light-colored and reflects a large share of the solar energy that hits it, which is why it has become so common in hot, high-sun regions like the Central Valley and northern Nevada. A reflective roof keeps membrane temperatures lower on summer afternoons and can reduce the cooling load on the space below. EPDM, in its traditional black form, absorbs heat instead — which works against you in a Sacramento or Reno summer, but can be a modest advantage in colder, cloudier settings. White EPDM exists, but reflectivity is where TPO usually earns its popularity in our region.

Then think about how the membranes go together. TPO seams are hot-air welded — the sheets are fused into a single monolithic layer, and a well-executed weld is generally the strongest part of the system. EPDM seams rely on tapes and adhesives, which modern practice has made very reliable, but which depend heavily on clean surfaces and careful workmanship. In both cases, the installer matters more than the brochure: most single-ply failures we're called to look at trace back to detailing at seams, penetrations, and terminations, not to the field membrane itself.

EPDM's long history is its strongest argument. It has decades of real-world performance behind it, it stays flexible in cold weather, and it tolerates building movement well. It's often a comfortable fit for roofs with lots of thermal cycling or where a dark, heat-absorbing surface isn't a penalty. TPO is a younger material, and formulations have evolved over the years — one more reason to insist on established manufacturers and installers who follow their specifications exactly.

Also consider what lives on your roof. Restaurants and buildings that exhaust grease or oils should know that certain chemicals attack some membranes more than others, and rooftop equipment means foot traffic, which means walkway pads and protection details regardless of membrane. If your roof doubles as a work platform for HVAC technicians, plan for that from day one.

The honest summary: in most of California and Nevada's hot, high-UV climate zones, a reflective TPO system is the default conversation-starter, and EPDM remains a strong choice where its track record, flexibility, or specific site conditions favor it. Either one, installed well over sound insulation and detailed correctly, will serve a building for many years. Either one, installed poorly, will not. Get a qualified contractor to evaluate your specific roof before deciding.

Article 02 — Maintenance

Five warning signs your commercial roof needs attention before winter

Most roof failures don't announce themselves in the rain. They telegraph their intentions months ahead — in small ways that are easy to spot from the roof and easy to miss from the parking lot. Before the wet season arrives, here are the five signs facilities teams should be looking for.

1. Ponding water. Low-slope roofs are designed to drain, not to hold water. If you can still see standing water or the telltale dirt rings it leaves behind days after a rain, the roof is telling you its drainage isn't working — clogged drains, crushed insulation, or deflection in the structure. Ponding accelerates membrane aging, adds weight, and turns a small membrane defect into an interior leak.

2. Blisters, bubbles, and soft spots. Raised areas in the membrane usually mean moisture or air trapped underneath, and a spongy feel underfoot often means wet insulation. Wet insulation doesn't dry out on its own — it loses its insulating value and quietly spreads the damage. The earlier it's found and isolated, the smaller the repair.

3. Open or lifting seams and flashings. The field of a membrane rarely fails first. The edges do — seams, penetrations, curbs, and the flashings around rooftop units. Look for lifted edges, cracked sealant, and gaps where the membrane meets walls or equipment. These are the entry points winter rain will find first, and they are usually inexpensive to correct when caught early.

4. Debris and clogged drains. Leaves, tennis balls, abandoned equipment, and construction leftovers all end up on roofs, and they all end up at the drains. A single blocked drain before a major storm can put a surprising amount of water on a roof that was never meant to hold it. A fall cleanup walk — clearing drains, scuppers, and gutters — is the cheapest insurance a building owner can buy.

5. Stains on the ceiling below. By the time water reaches an interior ceiling tile, it has usually been traveling inside the roof assembly for a while — often entering far from where it shows up. Don't just replace the tile. Treat every interior stain as a prompt to find the actual entry point on the roof before winter multiplies it.

The common thread: every one of these problems is dramatically cheaper to fix in October than in January, when crews are working between storms and the interior damage is already done. If any of these signs sound familiar — or if nobody has walked your roof since last winter — schedule an inspection before the weather makes the decision for you.

Article 03 — Planning

Restoration, re-roof, or replace: how to think about an aging low-slope roof

Every low-slope roof eventually reaches the point where patch-and-repair stops making sense. When it does, owners face three broad paths — restoration, re-cover, or full replacement — and the right one depends on what's actually happening inside the roof assembly, not just what the surface looks like.

Restoration means keeping the existing membrane and renewing its surface, most often with a fluid-applied coating system after seams and details are repaired. It's the lightest-touch option: less disruption, less tear-off waste, and generally the lowest cost of the three. But restoration only works on a roof that is fundamentally sound — dry insulation, an adhered and intact membrane, and details that can be brought back to good condition. Coating over a wet or failing roof buys appearance, not performance.

Re-covering (a re-roof over the existing system) installs a new membrane — often with a separation or insulation layer — on top of the old roof. It avoids most tear-off cost and disruption while delivering a genuinely new waterproofing layer. The catches: building codes generally limit how many roof systems can be stacked on a structure, the existing roof must be dry enough to bury, and everything hidden below the new membrane stays hidden. A moisture survey before committing is not optional — trapped wet insulation under a new roof is a problem you've now paid to conceal.

Full replacement — tearing off down to the deck and building the assembly new — is the most disruptive and typically the most expensive path, and it's also the only one that resets the clock completely. It's the right call when insulation is saturated, when the deck needs repair, when the code-allowed number of roofs is already in place, or when you want to upgrade insulation and drainage rather than inherit the old roof's compromises. It's also the moment to fix the slope and drainage problems that aged the last roof prematurely.

How do you choose? Start with information, not preference. A proper evaluation — core cuts, a moisture scan, a hard look at seams, flashings, and drainage, and a review of how long you intend to hold the building — turns the decision from guesswork into arithmetic. A roof on a building you'll sell in a few years and a roof on a hospital you'll operate for decades justify different answers, even if the two roofs look identical from above.

Be wary of anyone who prescribes before diagnosing. A contractor who recommends full replacement without core cuts, or a coating over a roof nobody has moisture-tested, is guessing with your money. The sequence should always be: inspect, test, then decide.
